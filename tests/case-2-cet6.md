For most of the last century, the price of a product was the only number shoppers really cared about. That is no longer quite true. A growing share of buyers now ask a second question before they pay: how long will this last, and can it be repaired when it fails?

The change has been driven by several forces at once. Governments in Europe have begun to require that appliances carry a repairability score, much like the energy labels that appeared on refrigerators in the 1990s. Online communities publish teardown reports that reveal which devices are glued shut and which can be opened with a single screwdriver. Meanwhile, the rising cost of living has made a ten-year laptop suddenly look cheaper than a three-year one.

Manufacturers have responded in two very different ways. Some have redesigned their products around replaceable parts, accepting slightly higher production costs in exchange for a longer selling life. Others have simply added a repair option to their marketing language while keeping the same sealed designs, a practice that consumer groups describe as cosmetic.

Consumers, for their part, are not entirely consistent. Surveys show strong support for repairable goods, yet the same shoppers routinely choose the cheapest model on the shelf when the two options sit side by side. Convenience, it seems, still outweighs principle at the moment of payment, which is why campaigners argue that labels and rules matter more than good intentions.

It is tempting to conclude that the repair economy has already won. In reality, the numbers remain modest. Repairable devices still occupy a small corner of the market, and many independent repair shops report that spare parts are becoming harder, not easier, to obtain.

What has genuinely changed is the conversation. Price alone no longer settles a purchase, and companies now expect to be asked how their products will age. That shift may matter more than any single regulation.

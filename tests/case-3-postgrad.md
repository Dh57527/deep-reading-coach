Digital designers have long treated friction as an enemy. Every extra click, every required decision, every moment of hesitation is counted as a defect to be removed, and the products that win praise are usually the ones that ask the least of their users. This attitude has produced interfaces of remarkable smoothness, and it has also produced a generation of users who cannot explain how the systems they depend on actually work.

The case against friction rests on a simple assumption: that effort is a cost and nothing more. If a task can be completed in three seconds rather than thirty, the reasoning goes, then it has been improved by a factor of ten. Under this logic, the ideal interface would anticipate every need before it is articulated, leaving the user with nothing to do but accept the result.

Yet a growing body of research suggests that the effort a task demands is not merely a cost. When people are forced to choose among options, to compare evidence and to commit to a decision, they build a model of the situation in their heads, and that model is what allows them to notice when something has gone wrong. Remove the effort, and the model never forms. The user remains competent at operating the interface and incompetent at understanding the world the interface describes.

Consider the skill of navigation. Drivers who learned to read a paper map developed a rough sense of distance and direction that allowed them to recover after a wrong turn. Drivers who rely entirely on spoken instructions often cannot say which way is north, and when the signal fails they stop, not because the route is difficult, but because they have no representation of it. The same pattern appears in medical software that pre-selects likely diagnoses, in financial apps that summarise a portfolio into a single score, and in writing tools that complete sentences before the writer has decided what to say.

None of this is an argument for nostalgia. Much friction is genuinely wasteful: forms that demand the same information twice, menus that hide the setting a user needs, confirmations that exist only to protect the company from liability. Defenders of smooth design are right that these obstacles deserve to disappear, and the productivity gains from their removal are real and measurable.

What deserves scrutiny is a different kind of friction, the kind that slows a person down at the moment when understanding is being formed. A well-designed system might deliberately ask the user to confirm a choice, to look at the evidence behind a recommendation, or to see the route before it is followed. Such interventions cost seconds and return something harder to measure: a user who can still act when the automation is switched off.

Designing deliberate friction is therefore less an engineering problem than a question about what a product wants its users to become. A system that removes every opportunity for thought is efficient in the short term and fragile in the long term, because it produces dependence rather than capability. The interesting design question is no longer how few steps a task can be reduced to, but which steps are worth keeping.
